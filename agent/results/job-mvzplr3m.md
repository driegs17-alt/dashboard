## Draft outline (template, edit freely)

**Working title:** [Interviewee's first name or role]'s [decade/event]: [what the story shows about bigger US history]

### I. Introduction
- **Hook:** one vivid moment from the interview (paraphrase or short quote). → *Use your strongest "I remember when…" story.*
- **Who and when:** [first name or role, e.g. "my grandmother"], born around [year], grew up in [region, kind of town], spoke with me on [month/year] about [era/event].
- **Why this person:** how their life connects to [event/era] from class.
- **Thesis:** pick one option below and make it yours.

**Thesis options (fill in the brackets):**
1. **Confirms + complicates:** "[Name]'s memories of [event] support the textbook account of [big change], but also show that [ordinary people / their region / their group] experienced it as [something the standard account misses]."
2. **Local vs. national:** "Although [event] is usually told as a national story about [X], [Name]'s experience in [place] shows it was shaped by [local factor: race, class, region, gender, religion, work]."
3. **Memory as evidence:** "[Name]'s account of [event] reveals as much about how Americans *remember* [era] as about what happened, since [hindsight / later events / family values] shaped which details stood out."
4. **Change over time:** "[Name]'s life from [year] to [year] traces the shift from [earlier condition] to [later condition], showing how [big force: suburbanization, civil rights, the Cold War, deindustrialization, immigration, technology] reached into everyday life."

### II. Historical context (likely the "Context" part of the eLC item)
- **Big picture:** what was happening nationally in [era]: 2-3 key events, laws or trends. → *See the context bank below.*
- **Course tie-in:** connect to [lecture topic / textbook chapter / required reading from Dr. Wolf].
- **Local/personal setting:** what [state/town/community] was like then (jobs, segregation, military base, farm vs. city, etc.).
- **Source(s):** [textbook chapter + 1-2 scholarly or primary sources, if required].

### III. Interviewee background
- Family, where they grew up, schooling, work, military service if any.
- What position they were in to see the event (participant, bystander, child, worker, soldier, parent).
- One sentence on your relationship and how you set up the interview (in person/phone, how long, recorded or notes).

### IV. Theme 1: [e.g. "Work and money," "Race and community," "War at home"]
- **Claim:** what this part of the interview shows.
- **Evidence:** *Use the quote/story about [topic].*
- **History link:** how it fits or pushes against [context point from II].
- **So what:** what it adds to the thesis.

### V. Theme 2: [second big topic]
- Same four steps: claim, interview evidence, history link, so what.
- *Use a story where they describe a change: "before" vs. "after."*

### VI. Theme 3 (optional, if length allows): [third topic or a turning point]
- Same four steps.
- *Good spot for a moment where their memory differs from the "official" story.*

### VII. Oral history as a source (analysis)
- **Strengths:** first-hand detail, emotion, everyday life that documents leave out.
- **Limits:** memory fades and gets reshaped by later events; the person may smooth over painful parts; your questions shaped the answers.
- **Your check:** where you compared their account to [textbook / news / another source] and what matched or didn't.
- Optional reading tie-in: Alessandro Portelli, "What Makes Oral History Different" (argues memory "errors" are themselves evidence of meaning). Use only if Dr. Wolf allows outside sources.

### VIII. Conclusion
- Restate the thesis in new words.
- What this one life shows about [era] that a textbook alone wouldn't.
- Close with a final image or quote from the interview, or what you took away.

### Works cited / notes
- The interview: "[Name or role], interview by [your name], [location], [date]." (Chicago note style; check what Dr. Wolf wants.)
- Textbook / course readings: [list].
- Any outside sources: [list].
